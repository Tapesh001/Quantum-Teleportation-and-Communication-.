What does this project investigate?

Imagine Alice has a particle prepared in a particular quantum state. Bob already has a different particle in his lab. Their goal is for Bob’s particle to acquire the state that Alice prepared, even if neither person knows exactly what that state is. That transfer of a state is called quantum teleportation.

Our research question: How accurately can Bob recover Alice’s state when their shared quantum resource is imperfect and their ordinary messages can arrive with errors? This project uses a laptop to calculate what would happen under those conditions. You choose an input, change the noise, and compare the predicted output with the original.

1 · Read a qubit on the Bloch sphere

A classical bit records 0 or 1. A qubit can be prepared in a superposition, written a|0⟩ + b|1⟩. The amplitudes a and b determine measurement probabilities and carry a relative phase. For example, |+⟩ and |−⟩ each give 0 and 1 equally often when measured along the Z axis, yet a measurement along X can tell these two states apart. A successful transfer must preserve that phase as well as the 0/1 probabilities.

In the simulator, θ moves the input arrow from the north pole (|0⟩) to the south pole (|1⟩). φ moves it around the vertical axis. A pure state reaches the surface. An arrow ending inside the sphere represents a mixed state; at the centre, all measurement axes give equally likely outcomes. Alice’s arrow is the saved input for comparison. Select Align views to compare Bob’s arrow from the same angle.

2 · Why teleportation needs two kinds of communication
  1. Prepare and share. Alice has the input qubit q0. She and Bob also share an entangled pair, q1 and q2. In the ideal circuit the pair is in the Bell state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2. This joint state cannot be assigned as two independent pure states.
  2. Alice measures. A controlled flip (CNOT) and a Hadamard gate (H) allow Alice to make a joint Bell measurement on q0 and q1. She gets one of four equally likely two-bit outcomes. This consumes the original input as a separate copy.
  3. Alice sends two ordinary bits. They identify which correction Bob needs. They do not contain the amplitudes of the input qubit. Until the message arrives, averaging over the possible outcomes gives Bob a fully mixed local state. He cannot use entanglement alone to read Alice’s input or signal faster than light.
  4. Bob corrects q2. The second received bit controls X, which swaps |0⟩ and |1⟩. The first controls Z, which changes the sign of the |1⟩ amplitude. The messages 00, 01, 10 and 11 call for no gate, X, Z and X then Z, respectively. With an ideal pair and reliable bits, each branch recovers the input.

Try one message. Suppose the input was |1⟩ and Alice measured 01. In our circuit Bob then has |0⟩. If he receives 01, X gives him |1⟩. If the second bit flips in transit, he receives 00, skips X and keeps |0⟩. The same measurement gave different outputs because the classical message changed. Explore the possibilities in the State simulator. Protocol source: Bennett et al. (1993).

3 · What the selected research paper actually showed

Why and what. In 1997, Bouwmeester and colleagues asked whether the theoretical protocol could transfer a real photon’s polarization state onto another photon. Polarization describes how light’s electric field is oriented and can encode a qubit.

How. The team generated photon pairs in a nonlinear crystal. Alice’s input photon and her member of the entangled pair met at a beam splitter. A particular pair of detector clicks identified one Bell outcome. For those selected events, the researchers checked Bob’s photon with polarization filters. A fourth photon provided a trigger for the fourfold-coincidence measurements.

How to read the evidence. Figure 5 plots four-detector coincidences against the relative arrival delay at Alice’s beam splitter. Near temporal overlap, counts dipped for a filter orthogonal to the prepared polarization, while counts for the matching filter stayed roughly level. The authors reported 70% ± 3% visibility without background subtraction. Visibility measures the contrast of that dip. It is a different quantity from the fraction of photons delivered or the fidelity displayed by this software.

What carries over. The paper provided evidence for conditional state transfer using a selected detection outcome. Our model includes all four Bell outcomes and calculates their corrected outputs. It lets us vary resource quality and message reliability independently; it does not model optical loss, detector clicks or the measured visibility. Bouwmeester et al., Experimental quantum teleportation (1997), Fig. 5 and pp. 578–579.

4 · Reproduce a result from the project report

Resource mixing p replaces a fraction p of the ideal Bell resource with completely mixed two-qubit noise. Bit error e is the chance that each classical bit flips. Fidelity scores the overlap between Bob’s output and the target input: 1 is an exact match; a fully mixed qubit scores ½ against any pure target.

5 · The project extension: do the two errors happen together?

Select Try 10% comparison. With p = 0 and e = 0.10, independent errors leave both message bits intact with probability 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81. Perfectly linked errors flip both bits together in 10% of messages and leave both intact in 90%. Each individual bit has the same reliability in the two models.

The |1⟩ input scores 0.900 in both cases. Across six inputs, however, the means are 0.873333 (independent) and 0.933333 (linked). A double flip produces a Pauli error equivalent to Y up to a global phase, so the two Y-axis test states survive it. This is the report’s proposed correlated-error investigation, now implemented in the lab.

For the mathematical check, let r be the probability neither message bit flips. On the ideal-resource part, a correct message gives fidelity 1. A wrong Pauli correction preserves two of the six test states, giving mean ⅓. Mixing in the completely noisy resource contributes ½. With corrections enabled the prediction is F̄ = (1 − p)(1 + 2r)/3 + p/2. The engine computes the output by following and weighting the branches; the formula gives an independent check.
