What does this project investigate?

Imagine Alice has a particle prepared in a particular quantum state. Bob already has a different particle in his lab. Their goal is for Bob’s particle to acquire the state that Alice prepared, even if neither person knows exactly what that state is. That transfer of a state is called quantum teleportation.

Our research question: How accurately can Bob recover Alice’s state when their shared quantum resource is imperfect and their ordinary messages can arrive with errors? This project uses a laptop to calculate what would happen under those conditions. You choose an input, change the noise, and compare the predicted output with the original.

Software Guide

If this is your first visit, start with one question: Can Bob recover exactly the state Alice prepared? Give him an ideal connection first. Once you have seen a perfect transfer, changing just one setting makes the effect of noise much easier to spot.

Open the lab on your laptop

  1. Download the project ZIP and choose Extract All. Keep the extracted files together. You need Python 3.8 or newer and a modern browser.
  2. On Windows, open the extracted folder and double-click Launch_Model.cmd. Alternatively, open a terminal in the folder containing run.py and enter python run.py (or python3 run.py on macOS or Linux).
  3. Keep the terminal open while you work. Python runs the calculations on your laptop; the browser displays them. If a tab doesn’t open, copy the full address printed in the terminal into your browser. It starts with http://127.0.0.1: and its port can change each time you launch.

When you finish, press Ctrl + C in the terminal to stop the server. Your saved history stays on the laptop. On your next visit, launch the software again and use the newly printed address.

Your first calculation

Overview gives you the latest scores and a picture of the transfer. Open State simulator, enter 90 for both angles, choose Independent, and click Ideal. This preset sets p and e to zero, enables Bob’s corrections, and calculates immediately. You should see 1.000 for both fidelities. Click Align views: both arrows point along +Y. That is your starting result.

Now click Moderate noise. Alice’s input stays the same. Bob’s arrow becomes shorter, the input score falls to 0.756, and the six-state mean reads 0.799 when rounded. Both scores describe the same experiment. The first tells you how well Bob recovered the one state you chose; the second repeats the calculation for six different directions and averages their scores.

How the settings become a result

When you press Calculate, the browser sends the current settings to the local Python engine. The engine prepares Alice’s state and the shared pair, applies the circuit gates, and follows each of Alice’s four possible measurement outcomes. For each outcome it asks which message Bob could receive, how probable that message is, and which correction he would apply. It weights each possible output by its probability and combines them into Bob’s density matrix. That matrix supplies the gold arrow and the input fidelity. Chapter 6 explains the arithmetic in more detail.

You can inspect the individual pieces. Read the circuit from left to right: the top two wires belong to Alice and the bottom quantum wire belongs to Bob. In Classical message, pick Alice’s outcome and read each possible received message and its probability. Send these bits once draws one transmission. With noise on, two clicks can give different receipts while the fidelity stays the same: the receipt describes one draw, while the fidelity already includes every possible draw.

Expand State details and six-state fidelities for the numbers behind the picture. The six scores reveal which inputs suffer most. The two diagonal entries of Bob’s matrix give his Z-measurement probabilities. The separate four probabilities at the bottom belong to Alice’s outcomes. Alice can have four equally likely outcomes even when Bob recovers the input perfectly.

1 · Read a qubit on the Bloch sphere

A classical bit records 0 or 1. A qubit can be prepared in a superposition, written a|0⟩ + b|1⟩. The amplitudes a and b determine measurement probabilities and carry a relative phase. For example, |+⟩ and |−⟩ each give 0 and 1 equally often when measured along the Z axis, yet a measurement along X can tell these two states apart. A successful transfer must preserve that phase as well as the 0/1 probabilities.

In the simulator, θ moves the input arrow from the north pole (|0⟩) to the south pole (|1⟩). φ moves it around the vertical axis. A pure state reaches the surface. An arrow ending inside the sphere represents a mixed state; at the centre, all measurement axes give equally likely outcomes. Alice’s arrow is the saved input for comparison. Select Align views to compare Bob’s arrow from the same angle.

What does a measurement tell Alice?

Read the symbol |0⟩ aloud as “zero.” The brackets mean it is a quantum state. If Alice prepares |0⟩ and measures along Z, she gets 0 every time. If she prepares |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2, she gets 0 on about half of many identically prepared trials. A single 0 tells her very little: either preparation could have produced it. That is why “measure it, phone Bob, and tell him what to prepare” cannot perfectly transfer an arbitrary unknown qubit.

The simulator lets you choose the state because you are setting up and scoring the experiment. Bob’s correction rule has no access to your θ or φ. You can turn the input arrow to any direction and use exactly the same circuit.

Translate the controls into a state

The software uses a = cos(θ/2) and b = exp(iφ) sin(θ/2), converting the angles you enter from degrees. Squaring the magnitudes gives the probabilities |a|² and |b|², which add to one. The factor exp(iφ) sets the relative phase. Here i is the imaginary unit, with i² = −1.

2 · Why teleportation needs two kinds of communication
  1. Prepare and share. Alice has the input qubit q0. She and Bob also share an entangled pair, q1 and q2. In the ideal circuit the pair is in the Bell state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2. This joint state cannot be assigned as two independent pure states.
  2. Alice measures. A controlled flip (CNOT) and a Hadamard gate (H) allow Alice to make a joint Bell measurement on q0 and q1. She gets one of four equally likely two-bit outcomes. This consumes the original input as a separate copy.
  3. Alice sends two ordinary bits. They identify which correction Bob needs. They do not contain the amplitudes of the input qubit. Until the message arrives, averaging over the possible outcomes gives Bob a fully mixed local state. He cannot use entanglement alone to read Alice’s input or signal faster than light.
  4. Bob corrects q2. The second received bit controls X, which swaps |0⟩ and |1⟩. The first controls Z, which changes the sign of the |1⟩ amplitude. The messages 00, 01, 10 and 11 call for no gate, X, Z and X then Z, respectively. With an ideal pair and reliable bits, each branch recovers the input.

Try one message. Suppose the input was |1⟩ and Alice measured 01. In our circuit Bob then has |0⟩. If he receives 01, X gives him |1⟩. If the second bit flips in transit, he receives 00, skips X and keeps |0⟩. The same measurement gave different outputs because the classical message changed. Explore the possibilities in the State simulator. Protocol source: Bennett et al. (1993).

Follow an unknown state through all four branches

Let Alice’s input be a|0⟩ + b|1⟩. Preparing the pair applies H to q1 and then CNOT from q1 to q2. H turns |0⟩ into an equal superposition; CNOT flips its target only when its control is 1. That gives the pair its two terms, |00⟩ and |11⟩. Alice then uses a second CNOT, this time from q0 to q1, and H on q0. These last two gates convert the joint measurement into two ordinary 0/1 readouts.

This table assumes an ideal Bell pair. Each row occurs with probability ¼, whatever a and b are. Alice could get 01 whether she prepared north, south, or somewhere in between. Those two bits tell Bob how to undo an operation, which is why he can recover amplitudes he never learned.

Where is the original afterwards? Alice has two measured bits; Bob has the recovered quantum state. Alice cannot keep a perfect spare copy of an arbitrary unknown input. The entangled pair has also been used up. Transferring another qubit requires a fresh pair and another message.

3 · What the selected research paper actually showed

Why and what. In 1997, Bouwmeester and colleagues asked whether the theoretical protocol could transfer a real photon’s polarization state onto another photon. Polarization describes how light’s electric field is oriented and can encode a qubit.

How. The team generated photon pairs in a nonlinear crystal. Alice’s input photon and her member of the entangled pair met at a beam splitter. A particular pair of detector clicks identified one Bell outcome. For those selected events, the researchers checked Bob’s photon with polarization filters. A fourth photon provided a trigger for the fourfold-coincidence measurements.

How to read the evidence. Figure 5 plots four-detector coincidences against the relative arrival delay at Alice’s beam splitter. Near temporal overlap, counts dipped for a filter orthogonal to the prepared polarization, while counts for the matching filter stayed roughly level. The authors reported 70% ± 3% visibility without background subtraction. Visibility measures the contrast of that dip. It is a different quantity from the fraction of photons delivered or the fidelity displayed by this software.

What carries over. The paper provided evidence for conditional state transfer using a selected detection outcome. Our model includes all four Bell outcomes and calculates their corrected outputs. It lets us vary resource quality and message reliability independently; it does not model optical loss, detector clicks or the measured visibility. Bouwmeester et al., Experimental quantum teleportation (1997), Fig. 5 and pp. 578–579.

What would have counted as weak evidence?

Imagine testing only horizontal and vertical polarization. A device that simply measured in that basis and reproduced the answer could pass both tests. Trying inputs polarized in other directions probes more of the state. In the fourfold measurements, the paper examined +45° and +90° inputs. “Fourfold” means that four detectors registered a coincidence within the experiment’s timing conditions. The fourth detection helped reject unwanted events.

The optical setup identified one particular Bell outcome. Ideally that outcome accounts for one quarter of trials before losses. The 70% visibility answers a different question: how much did the selected coincidence counts dip as the photon arrivals were brought into overlap? Neither number can be entered as p to recreate the paper. To connect the two experiments quantitatively, we would also need a model of the source, arrival times, detector efficiency and which events were retained.

4 · Reproduce a result from the project report

Resource mixing p replaces a fraction p of the ideal Bell resource with completely mixed two-qubit noise. Bit error e is the chance that each classical bit flips. Fidelity scores the overlap between Bob’s output and the target input: 1 is an exact match; a fully mixed qubit scores ½ against any pure target.

Read Bob’s matrix as well as his arrow

A single arrow direction is enough for a perfectly prepared pure state. With unknown errors, Bob can receive a mixture of different states. The density matrix ρ collects everything needed to predict measurements on that mixture. Its two diagonal entries give the probabilities of measuring 0 and 1 along Z. The off-diagonal entries carry the phase information needed for X and Y measurements.

At the moderate setting above, the diagonal entries are both 0.5 and Bob’s vector is (0, 0.512, 0). Equal Z probabilities alone would miss the surviving preference for +Y. Measuring along the original +Y direction succeeds with probability (1 + 0.512)/2 = 0.756. That is what “input fidelity” means for this pure target. A completely mixed output would give 0.5 along every direction.

For any pure input with unit Bloch vector s and output vector v, the same rule is F = (1 + s·v)/2. The dot product rewards alignment. A pure arrow pointing in exactly the opposite direction gives zero, so 0.5 is the fully mixed reference point rather than the lowest possible fidelity.

5 · The project extension: do the two errors happen together?

Select Try 10% comparison. With p = 0 and e = 0.10, independent errors leave both message bits intact with probability 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81. Perfectly linked errors flip both bits together in 10% of messages and leave both intact in 90%. Each individual bit has the same reliability in the two models.

The |1⟩ input scores 0.900 in both cases. Across six inputs, however, the means are 0.873333 (independent) and 0.933333 (linked). A double flip produces a Pauli error equivalent to Y up to a global phase, so the two Y-axis test states survive it. This is the report’s proposed correlated-error investigation, now implemented in the lab.

For the mathematical check, let r be the probability neither message bit flips. On the ideal-resource part, a correct message gives fidelity 1. A wrong Pauli correction preserves two of the six test states, giving mean ⅓. Mixing in the completely noisy resource contributes ½. With corrections enabled the prediction is F̄ = (1 − p)(1 + 2r)/3 + p/2. The engine computes the output by following and weighting the branches; the formula gives an independent check.

Work through 100 messages

At e = 0.10, independent errors give expected counts of 81 intact messages, 9 with just the first bit flipped, 9 with just the second flipped, and 1 with both flipped per 100 transmissions. Linked errors give 90 intact and 10 with both flipped. These are long-run averages; any individual batch can differ.

Why does |1⟩ hide the difference? An extra Z changes |1⟩ into −|1⟩. Multiplying the whole state by −1 is an overall phase, which leaves every measurement probability unchanged. An extra X sends it to |0⟩ instead. For this input, fidelity depends on whether the X-control bit was wrong, and that bit flips 10% of the time in either model. Try a +Y input and the pattern matters: at p = 0 the fidelities are 0.820 for independent errors and 1.000 for perfectly linked errors.

6 · How a laptop produces the numbers

Three qubits have eight computational-basis configurations, from |000⟩ to |111⟩ in the order q0, q1, q2. Python stores eight complex amplitudes for each pure preparation it follows. A gate changes the amplitudes; the joint measurement divides them into four possible outcomes. For each outcome, the engine takes Bob’s two amplitudes, applies the correction indicated by the received bits and adds the resulting density matrix with the proper weight.

There are two kinds of uncertainty in that sum. The shared resource is a Bell pair with weight 1 − p plus a completely mixed two-qubit contribution with weight p. The latter can be represented as equal contributions from 00, 01, 10 and 11. Independently of the resource preparation, the selected message model supplies the probabilities of the four flip patterns. Summing these branches gives an exact ensemble prediction up to numerical rounding.

Calculate therefore returns the same matrix and fidelity each time for the same settings. Send these bits once answers a smaller question: given Alice’s selected outcome, which message might arrive on this one transmission? That button samples an error pattern. One lucky arrival does not establish the average performance of the channel.

Useful checks have answers you can predict before pressing Calculate. With p = e = 0 and corrections enabled, any input has fidelity 1. Switch off corrections: the four possible branches average to the centre and fidelity becomes ½. Turn corrections back on and set p = 1: a completely mixed shared resource again gives ½. These cases, together with checks of the density matrix and the analytic mean, help catch mistakes such as swapping which classical bit controls X.

7 · Run an investigation and explain your conclusion
  1. In State simulator, set θ = φ = 90°, choose Independent, enable corrections and select Ideal. Compare the arrows using Align views. Both fidelity scores should be 1.
  2. Open Noise laboratory and choose Moderate noise. Read the selected-input score and the six-state mean separately. Start the sweep: p goes from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.01 while the input and e stay fixed. The horizontal axis compares resource preparations. Camera rotation changes only the viewing angle.
  3. Move the cursor across the graph. The two preview values compare error patterns at the same p. The gold point marks the selected calculation. At e = 0.10 the independent curve crosses ⅔ near p = 0.554. To see why, substitute r = 0.81 into the mean formula: F̄ ≈ 0.87333 − 0.37333p. At the crossing, the protocol matches the classical benchmark on average.
  4. Use Try 10% comparison to isolate the classical channel. Keep p, e and the input fixed when comparing the two rows. Export CSV saves six input states at each of 101 p values, or 606 rows. Use History to recover the settings behind a result you want to discuss.

What can we conclude? In this model, both the quality of the shared pair and the reliability of the two-bit message determine the quality of state transfer. The way the two errors occur together matters even at equal individual error rates. A single preferred input can hide that effect.

Where does that conclusion stop? The lab predicts the quality of the output under specified assumptions. It cannot tell us how many photons a receiver will detect per second, how far they will travel, or how long a memory will retain the state. In a network, Bob may need to store his qubit while awaiting Alice’s signal. A natural next experiment would add a storage time and measure how waiting changes fidelity while keeping the original p and e fixed.
